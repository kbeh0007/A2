Europe: The Home of Formula 1

"More than half of all F1 races have been run on European soil."

Europe has dominated F1's calendar since 1950 — and for good reason. The sport was born here: the first World Championship race was held at Silverstone in 1950, and the governing body (FIA) is headquartered in Paris. Beyond history, circuits like Monaco, Monza, Spa-Francorchamps and Silverstone sit inside destinations that attract visitors well beyond race weekend — fans combine the Grand Prix with tourism, making European races commercially sustainable in ways that purpose-built circuits elsewhere struggle to match. The gold, silver and bronze dots on the right rank nations by total Grands Prix hosted. Italy leads the world with over 100 races.

Speed and Strategy on Track

"Starting from pole gives you the best chance — but the grid is not destiny."

Qualifying lap times at Silverstone tell the story of F1's regulation cycles. Times were fastest in 2003–2005 when single-lap qualifying — one flying lap on fresh tyres, no traffic — coincided with the final V10 season. Times rose permanently from 2010 when Silverstone added a new 805-metre Arena infield section.

Does where you start determine where you finish?

Each dot represents a grid-to-finish combination across all races since 2010. The Pole → Win cluster at the top-left is the densest of all — starting first gives the best statistical chance of winning, but drivers from positions 5–10 still regularly reach the podium.

The Most Dramatic Season — 2021

"8 points. 21 races. One final lap. The most disputed title in modern F1."

Lewis Hamilton and Max Verstappen swapped the championship lead three times across 22 rounds in 2021, separated by fewer than 10 points for most of the year. Grey lines show the other contenders falling away as the battle became two-man.

Hover any point for round by round details.

Going into the final round at Abu Dhabi, Verstappen led Hamilton by just 8 points. Hamilton led the race and was on course to be champion — until a controversial late safety car decision gave Verstappen fresh tyres and one final lap to overtake. He did. Verstappen won his first World Drivers' Championship by exactly 8 points — one of the most dramatic and controversial finishes in F1 history.

Malaysia at Full Speed — 19 Years at Sepang

"From Schumacher's defiance to Verstappen's farewell — 19 races, 19 stories."

From Schumacher's comeback in 1999 to Verstappen's farewell win in 2017, Sepang hosted some of F1's most iconic moments. Each row is one race, coloured by the winning constructor.

Hover any row for details.

1999 Schumacher returned from injury to help Ferrari secure a 1-2 finish. Both cars were disqualified then reinstated on appeal.
2001 A torrential tropical rainstorm mid-race turned Sepang into a survival test. Ferrari's strategic brilliance prevailed.
2009 The race was stopped after 31 laps due to extreme weather and declared under half points rules. Winner Jenson Button drove for Brawn GP — a team that appeared only this season after Honda withdrew from F1, sold for £1, and went on to win both championships.
2013 The infamous Multi 21 controversy — Vettel ignored team orders and overtook Webber, sparking a major internal Red Bull dispute.
2017 Verstappen won the final Malaysian GP, dedicating it to his father Jos. Malaysia's F1 chapter closed that afternoon.

F1 is Bigger Than Ever — But Malaysia Left Too Soon

"Malaysia left at a decade low. Since then, F1's audience has nearly doubled."

When Malaysia left in 2017, F1 viewership was at a decade low of just 352 million. Since then, driven by Netflix's Drive to Survive and a new generation of fans, the sport has grown in every measurable way. The shaded region marks the years Malaysia was on the calendar. Key inflection points are annotated on each panel — Malaysia's exit in 2017 and the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, which caused sharp drops in attendance and revenue while TV viewership held up as fans watched from home.

Can Malaysia Afford to Return?

"The sport Malaysia loves is now worth twice what it was when we left it."

F1 has never been more popular. The global fanbase exceeds 800 million, The global fanbase exceeds 800 million, race attendance is at record highs, and revenue has more than doubled since Malaysia left the calendar. Southeast Asia has no race since Singapore dominates the region. Yet Malaysia's Sports Minister confirmed in August 2025 that hosting fees are estimated at RM300 million per year, with a typical 3 to 5 year contract costing up to RM1.5 billion total. When Malaysia last hosted, Sepang rarely filled its 130,000 seat capacity. The question is not whether Malaysia loves F1. The question is whether the sport's new golden age changes the economics enough to justify the investment, and whether private funding could finally make it happen.
